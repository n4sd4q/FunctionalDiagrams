\section{Overview}\label{sec:overview}

\subsection{What the package does}

FunctionalDiagrams is a Mathematica package for the functional calculus of quantum field theory and many-body physics,
and for the Feynman diagrams that it generates. It has five layers.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An engine for functional derivatives} (sections~\ref{sec:expressions}--\ref{sec:derivatives}). Expressions
  are sums of integrals, over labels, of products of functions; some factors are Grassmann odd and their order matters.
  The engine differentiates such expressions with respect to commuting and anticommuting sources, with the product and
  chain rules, the Grassmann signs and rules declared for objects that depend on the sources implicitly; it integrates
  the delta functions that the derivatives produce, and it brings every term to a canonical form, in which terms that
  differ only by the names of their integration variables are recognized as equal and combined.
\item \textbf{Generating functionals and diagrams} (sections~\ref{sec:fields}--\ref{sec:classes}). Fields are
  derivatives with respect to their sources; expectation values follow by differentiating a generating functional, and
  for Gaussian functionals by Wick's theorem. Perturbation theory, connected and vacuum diagrams, irreducibility,
  skeletons and amputation are all built on the engine. The terms are diagrams: every integration variable is a vertex,
  every two-label factor a line.
\item \textbf{Functionals and their derivatives} (sections~\ref{sec:conventions}--\ref{sec:trees}). The functionals of
  many-body theory and field theory --- $W=\log Z$, the 1PI effective action $\Gamma[\bar\phi]$, the 2PI effective action
  $\Gamma[\bar\phi,G]$, the Luttinger--Ward functional $\Phi[G]$, the functional $\Phi[\mathcal G]$ of a superfield, Potthoff's
  functionals --- are objects of the package, written with inert traces of operators. Their derivatives with respect to
  sources, means and two-point objects are the self-energy and the irreducible vertex, the full vertex and the vertex
  functions, the fully irreducible vertex and the kernels of the two-particle channels; Bethe--Salpeter ladders, the
  parquet decomposition, the exact equations between full objects and the tree expansion of connected functions in
  vertex functions check and relate them.
\item \textbf{Pictures and formulas} (sections~\ref{sec:pictures}--\ref{sec:latex}). Every expression can be drawn, as
  Mathematica graphics or as TikZ code for \LaTeX{}, in a standard style computed from the diagram; formulas can be
  written as \LaTeX{}.
\item \textbf{Evaluation} (sections~\ref{sec:matsubara}--\ref{sec:qed}). For a translation-invariant system at finite
  temperature, the diagrams are evaluated in momentum and Matsubara frequency: momenta and frequencies are routed through
  the lines, the frequency sums are carried out by residues, the result is continued to real frequency, and its imaginary
  part is checked against the finite-temperature cutting rules. At zero temperature, in Euclidean space, the loop
  integrals of the diagrams are evaluated in dimensional regularization and expanded in $\varepsilon$, and the
  renormalization constants and renormalization-group functions follow (the $\overline{\rm MS}$ renormalization of
  $\phi^4$ theory to two loops). In QED at one loop, the diagrams with spinor and Lorentz indices become products and
  traces of Dirac matrices in $d$ dimensions, their loop integrals are done by Feynman parameters, and the electron
  self-energy, the vacuum polarization and the vertex give the renormalization constants, $\beta(e)$ and the form
  factors of the vertex.
\end{itemize}

The guiding principle is that everything is generated by functional derivatives. The user writes a functional --- $Z[J]$,
$W=\log Z$, $\Gamma[\bar\phi]$, $\Gamma[\bar\phi,G]$, $\Phi[G]$, $\Phi[\mathcal G]$ --- and obtains every object by
\fnref{FunctionalDerivative} with respect to sources, means and propagators (normal and anomalous); functions such as
\fnref{SelfEnergy}, \fnref{IrreducibleVertex}, \fnref{PerturbativeFullVertex}, \fnref{PerturbativeDensityVertex} or the option
\opt{Channel} are shortcuts for such derivatives, and this manual shows the derivative first and the shortcut as the
check. In particular, weights are never put in by hand: symmetry factors and fermionic signs come out of the algebra, and
the package only has to recognize when two terms are the same diagram.

\subsection{Requirements and installation}\label{sec:install}

The package is a single file, \pkg{FunctionalDiagrams.wl}, written for Mathematica~12.1 or later (for the
paclet manager that loads it).
The recommended installation is the paclet of a release, which also contains the showcase, this manual and the guided
tour:
\begin{code}
PacletInstall["/path/to/FunctionalDiagrams-2.4.0.paclet"]   (* once *)
Needs["FunctionalDiagrams`"]
\end{code}
(\pkg{PacletInstall} also takes the address of the paclet on the page of the release.) This manual describes
version~2.4.0, the latest release. A clone of the repository is a
paclet too, loaded by \pkg{PacletDirectoryLoad} of its folder and then \pkg{Needs}.
Without the paclet, load the file with
\begin{code}
Get["/path/to/FunctionalDiagrams/FunctionalDiagrams.wl"]
\end{code}
or copy it into the folder \pkg{Applications} of \pkg{\$UserBaseDirectory} and load it with
\begin{code}
Needs["FunctionalDiagrams`"]
\end{code}
The functions are in the context \pkg{FunctionalDiagrams`}; \pkg{?FunctionalDiagrams`*} lists them and \pkg{?Name}
shows their usage. \pkg{\$FunctionalDiagramsVersion} is the version, \pkg{"2.4.0"}. Loading the
package again in the same session starts afresh: the definitions of the package and its record of declarations are
cleared (attributes that the declarations gave to your own symbols, such as \pkg{Orderless} for a symmetric function,
remain).

When the package is loaded, it checks that none of its public names is already a symbol of the context \pkg{System`}
(a built-in function of a later version of Mathematica), which would be used in place of the package's function, and
issues \pkg{FunctionalDiagrams::clash} if one is. A name of the package that another package loaded in the same
session also defines, or that was typed before the package was loaded (a symbol in \pkg{Global`}), gives instead
Mathematica's own message \pkg{::shdw} (\pkg{DiagramPlot::shdw}, say): the function used is that of the context loaded
last, which comes first in \pkg{\$ContextPath}.
In a notebook the package then prints a banner two lines high: the logo, the attribution ``FunctionalDiagrams, by Nazzareno
Africani'' (which modified versions must keep), the version, the license, how to cite the package
(section~\ref{sec:citing}) and links to the repository and to this manual. In a script (\pkg{wolfram -script}) nothing
is printed.

In a notebook, loading the package also checks for a newer release, at most once a day: it asks GitHub
(\pkg{api.github.com}) for the latest release in the background, so that loading does not wait for the answer, and if
that release is newer than \pkg{\$FunctionalDiagramsVersion}, the banner gets a third line with its version, a link to
what is new and the \pkg{PacletInstall} line of its paclet, ready to copy. The date of the last check is kept in the
file \pkg{LastUpdateCheck.txt} of the folder \pkg{ApplicationData/FunctionalDiagrams} of \pkg{\$UserBaseDirectory}.
Every failure (no connection, a proxy, the rate limit of GitHub) is silent, and a script never checks, nor does a
session in which Mathematica may not use the Internet (\pkg{\$AllowInternet}). \pkg{\$FunctionalDiagramsUpdateCheck}
is \pkg{True}; set to \pkg{False} before the package is loaded, it turns the check off:
\begin{code}
$FunctionalDiagramsUpdateCheck = False;
Needs["FunctionalDiagrams`"]
\end{code}
(in the file \pkg{init.m} of the folder \pkg{Kernel} of \pkg{\$UserBaseDirectory}, for every session).

\begin{function}{FunctionalDiagramsUpdateCheck}
\syntax{FunctionalDiagramsUpdateCheck[]}
\end{function}
Asks GitHub for the latest release now, whatever the date of the last check and the value of
\pkg{\$FunctionalDiagramsUpdateCheck}, and waits for the answer (up to ten seconds). It gives
\pkg{<|"InstalledVersion" -> \dots, "LatestVersion" -> \dots, "InstallLine" -> \dots|>}: the version loaded, the
latest release and the line that installs its paclet, as a string; when the release is newer, it also prints that line.
When the release cannot be read it gives \pkg{\$Failed}, with the message \pkg{FunctionalDiagramsUpdateCheck::latest}.

\subsection{A first session}

The free fermion propagator $G_0$, a two-body interaction $V$, the Green function to first order, its self-energy and a
picture:
\begin{code}
DeclareGrassmannSource[eta, etabar];
DeclareField[psi, etabar]; DeclareField[psibar, eta, -1];
DeclareSymmetric[V]; DeclareDirectedPropagator[G0]; DeclareInteractionLine[V];
Zf = Exp[-IntegralOver[G0[x, y] etabar[x] ** eta[y], {x, y}]];
CoulombVertex = 1/2 IntegralOver[V[x, y] psibar[x] ** psibar[y] ** psi[y] ** psi[x], {x, y}];
greenFunction = -PerturbativeCorrelationFunction[Zf, {psi[x], psibar[y]}, CoulombVertex, 1]
DiagramPlot[greenFunction, {x, y}]
\end{code}
The output and the pictures of this session are in section~\ref{sec:perturbation}, where the same computation is
evaluated step by step.

\subsection{Scope and limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item The package computes with diagrams in the space of labels. A label stands for whatever the problem needs ---
  position, imaginary time, spin, orbital --- and an integral is the integral or sum over it. Nothing is assumed about
  the propagators and interactions beyond their declared symmetries. Its results are exact symbolic expressions, sums of
  integrals of products of functions, which can be translated into any representation; for translation-invariant systems
  at finite temperature the package does it itself (section~\ref{sec:matsubara}), with the frequency sums carried out and
  the momentum integrals left symbolic; at zero temperature it evaluates the loop integrals that reduce to nested
  one-loop ones in dimensional regularization (section~\ref{sec:dimreg}), and the one-loop diagrams of QED, with their
  Dirac algebra (section~\ref{sec:qed}). It is not a program for the numerical evaluation of Feynman integrals.
\item Expectation values can be computed in any generating functional, by differentiation. Perturbation theory is
  fastest around a Gaussian free generating functional (any number of bosonic and fermionic fields), by Wick's theorem;
  around any other $Z_0$ it works by differentiation, more slowly. The interaction is polynomial in the
  fields, with any number of vertices of any kind and coupling constants as symbols.
\item The number of diagrams grows factorially with the order. With Mathematica the electron gas to third order --- 87
  diagrams of the Green function, 94 of the full vertex --- takes a few seconds (in the showcase, \pkg{values.txt}: about 0.1~s for $G$ and a second for $F$); higher orders are limited by time
  and memory only.
\item The canonical form is exact: two terms get the same canonical form if and only if they are the same diagram (up to
  the renaming of integration variables, the declared symmetries and the reordering of Grassmann factors). The algorithm
  can be exponential for very symmetric terms, but it is fast for diagrams.
\item The pictures are computed by heuristic rules, designed for the diagrams of many-body theory (fermion lines with
  density-type vertices) and for trees of vertex functions; other diagrams are drawn by a general embedding, which is
  readable but not always as in a textbook.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Notation used in this manual}

Function names are in \pkg{typewriter}; \pkg{f[x, y]} is Mathematica input, $f(x,y)$ the corresponding mathematical
object. In the formulas, $\int_{z_1z_2}$ is \pkg{IntegralOver[\dots, \{z1, z2\}]}, the integral or sum over everything a
label stands for (position, imaginary time, spin, \dots). A source is written $J$ (bosonic) or $\eta$, $\bar\eta$
(Grassmann), a field $\phi$, $\psi$, $\bar\psi$. In diagrams, an arrow from $y$ to $x$ is a propagator $G(x,y)$, a wavy
line an interaction $V$.

\section{Expressions}\label{sec:expressions}

\subsection{Terms}

Every expression the package works with is a sum of \emph{terms}
\[
c\;\int_{z_1\cdots z_n}f\;\theta_1\theta_2\cdots\theta_k,
\]
in Mathematica \pkg{c IntegralOver[f GrassmannProduct[$\theta_1$, \dots, $\theta_k$], \{z1, \dots, zn\}]},
where $c$ does not depend on the integration variables, $f$ is a product of commuting factors (functions of labels,
symbols, numbers, functions of other expressions), and $\theta_1\cdots\theta_k$ is an ordered product of Grassmann-odd
factors. A term is a diagram: its two-label factors are lines between its labels, its integration variables are internal
vertices, its other labels are external points. Any expression built from these pieces with \pkg{Plus}, \pkg{Times},
\pkg{**}, powers and functions is accepted as input; the functions of the package expand it into terms internally and
return a canonical sum.

\subsection{Notation}

\begin{function}{IntegralOver}
\syntax{IntegralOver[f, \{x, y, \dots\}]}
\end{function}
The integral (or sum) of $f$ over the labels $x,y,\dots$, which stand for position, imaginary time, spin, orbital or any
other index. The integration variables must be symbols. Integrals can be nested, and the integrand can contain anything,
including other integrals in the arguments of functions. It is displayed as $\int_{x,y}f$. In results the integration
variables are canonical: \pkg{DummyVariable[1, k]}, displayed $z_k$, in the outermost integrals,
\pkg{DummyVariable[2, k]}, displayed $w_k$, in integrals inside them, and so on ($u$, $v$, $t$).

\end{function}

automatically against integration variables:
\excode{delta_integrated}
\exformula{delta_integrated}

\begin{function}{GrassmannProduct}
\syntax{GrassmannProduct[a, b, \dots]}
\syntax{a ** b ** \dots}
\end{function}
The ordered product of Grassmann-odd factors. Products entered with \pkg{**}, that is with

attached to them, and the heads declared with \fnref{DeclareGrassmannOdd} or \fnref{DeclareGrassmannGraded}. A product of
two odd factors entered with \pkg{Times} is ambiguous, since \pkg{Times} is commutative: it gives a message

factor squared, \pkg{eta[x]\^{}2}, vanishes. Commuting factors can be multiplied
either way. In canonical form the odd factors are sorted, with the sign of the permutation, and a product with a
repeated odd factor vanishes.

\syntax{DummyVariable[depth, k]}
\end{function}
The $k$-th canonical integration variable at nesting depth \pkg{depth}, displayed $z_k$, $w_k$, $u_k$, $v_k$, $t_k$ for

other symbol.

\section{Declarations}\label{sec:declarations}

Declarations tell the package which heads are sources, fields, symmetric or Grassmann-odd functions, which objects
depend on the sources implicitly, and how lines are drawn. They are global, they accumulate, and
\fnref{ResetDeclarations} removes them all. The declarations of heads warn, with the message \pkg{FunctionalDiagrams::builtin},
if a name is a built-in symbol of the Wolfram Language (\pkg{C}, \pkg{D}, \pkg{E}, \pkg{I}, \pkg{K}, \pkg{N}, \pkg{O}, \pkg{Gamma}, \pkg{Beta},
\pkg{GreenFunction}, \dots): the definitions of built-in symbols interfere with those of the package, and other names
must be used, such as \pkg{BareGreenFunction} and \pkg{DressedGreenFunction}.

\subsection{Sources and fields}

\begin{function}{DeclareSource}
\syntax{DeclareSource[J1, J2, \dots]}
\end{function}
Declares commuting (bosonic) source heads: \pkg{J[x]} is then differentiated by \fnref{FunctionalDerivative}, and set to
zero by \fnref{SetSourcesToZero}. A source can have any number of arguments, \pkg{J[x]} or \pkg{J[x, s]}; the derivative
of \pkg{J[x, s]} with respect to \pkg{J[y, t]} is \pkg{DeltaFunction[x, y] DeltaFunction[s, t]}.

\begin{function}{DeclareGrassmannSource}
\syntax{DeclareGrassmannSource[eta, etabar, \dots]}
\end{function}
Declares anticommuting (Grassmann) source heads. Products of Grassmann sources must be written with \pkg{**}.

\begin{function}{DeclareField}
\syntax{DeclareField[phi, J]}
\syntax{DeclareField[psibar, eta, -1]}
\end{function}
Associates the field \pkg{phi} with the source \pkg{J}: \pkg{phi[x]} stands for $\delta/\delta J(x)$, and with the third
argument $s$ for $s\,\delta/\delta J(x)$. For the fermionic convention of this manual,
\pkg{DeclareField[psi, etabar]} and \pkg{DeclareField[psibar, eta, -1]}: $\psi\leftrightarrow\delta/\delta\bar\eta$,
$\bar\psi\leftrightarrow-\delta/\delta\eta$. Fields attached to Grassmann sources are Grassmann odd. Fields are used in
\fnref{FieldDerivative}, in expectation values and in interactions (section~\ref{sec:perturbation}).

\subsection{Symmetries and Grassmann parity}

\begin{function}{DeclareSymmetric}
\syntax{DeclareSymmetric[h1, h2, \dots]}
\end{function}
Makes the heads totally symmetric in their arguments (they get the attribute \pkg{Orderless}): bosonic propagators,
density--density interactions, totally symmetric vertices. The canonical form takes the symmetry into account.

\exformula{symmetric}

\begin{function}{DeclareAntisymmetric}
\syntax{DeclareAntisymmetric[h1, h2, \dots]}
\end{function}
Makes the heads totally antisymmetric: the arguments are sorted with the sign of the permutation, and a function with a
repeated argument vanishes. In a pattern or a rule write \pkg{h[\_\_]}, never \pkg{h[\_, \_]}, which evaluates to 0. The
derivative with respect to an antisymmetric kernel is antisymmetrized (\fnref{FunctionalDerivative}), and the kernel of the
inverse of an antisymmetric operator, \pkg{OperatorKernel[h\^{}-1]}, is antisymmetric: so is the propagator of a superfield,
the Nambu matrix of $(\psi,\bar\psi)$ (\fnref{SuperfieldComponents}).

\syntax{DeclareGrassmannOdd[c1, c2, \dots]}
\end{function}
Declares heads of Grassmann-odd quantities that do not depend on the sources, such as fermion fields written explicitly
in an expression. They anticommute with each other and with the Grassmann sources.

\begin{function}{DeclareGrassmannGraded}
\syntax{DeclareGrassmannGraded[h1, h2, \dots]}
\end{function}
Declares heads whose Grassmann parity is the number of their arguments modulo 2 and which depend on the sources: their

\begin{function}{DeclareDiscreteIndexValues}
\syntax{DeclareDiscreteIndexValues[\{up, down, \dots\}]}
\end{function}
Declares symbols that are values of a discrete index rather than labels: \pkg{DeltaFunction[up, up]} is 1,
\pkg{DeltaFunction[up, down]} is 0, and the drawing and the cuts ignore them. Sum over such an index explicitly, with
\pkg{Sum}: for spin-$\frac12$ fermions, with the fields \pkg{psi[x, s]} and \pkg{psibar[x, s]},
\begin{code}
Zf = Exp[-Sum[IntegralOver[G0[x, y] etabar[x, s] ** eta[y, s], {x, y}],
    {s, {up, down}}]];
interaction = 1/2 Sum[IntegralOver[V[x, y] psibar[x, s] ** psibar[y, t] **
    psi[y, t] ** psi[x, s], {x, y}], {s, {up, down}}, {t, {up, down}}];
\end{code}
The external fields carry
explicit values, \pkg{psi[a, up]}, not a symbolic spin label. Do not integrate over a
spin label with \pkg{IntegralOver}: the closed loops would leave traces $\int_s\delta(s,s)$, which the classes of
diagrams do not count. Lines are functions of two labels: a propagator that depends on spin has one head per spin
(\pkg{Gup}, \pkg{Gdown}); a line head with more arguments is treated as a vertex, with the message
\pkg{FunctionalDiagrams::lineargs}. With one head for both spins (\pkg{G0 -> G} in \fnref{LuttingerWardFunctional}),
$\delta\Phi/\delta G$ is the sum of the derivatives with respect to $G_\uparrow$ and $G_\downarrow$, taken at
$G_\uparrow=G_\downarrow=G$: twice the self-energy of one spin, $(2s+1)\Sigma$. The self-energy of one spin, and the vertex
with given spins, are derivatives with respect to one head per spin, \pkg{\{G0up -> Gup, G0down -> Gdown\}}:
$\Sigma_\uparrow(x,y)=\delta\Phi/\delta G_\uparrow(y,x)$, $\Gamma^0_{\uparrow\downarrow}=\delta\Sigma_\uparrow/\delta G_\downarrow$ (the direct
interaction only). A field without the spin index of its source (\pkg{psi[x]} when $Z_0$ holds \pkg{eta[x, s]}) has vanishing
expectation values: the message \pkg{FunctionalDiagrams::fieldarity}.

\subsection{Implicit dependence on the sources}

\begin{function}{DeclareFunctionalDerivative}
\syntax{DeclareFunctionalDerivative[object[a\_, \dots], source[c\_], rhs]}
\end{function}
Declares $\delta\,\mathrm{object}/\delta\,\mathrm{source}=\mathrm{rhs}$ for an object that depends on the sources implicitly,
for instance a propagator, a mean field or a vertex function in the presence of a source. The first two arguments are
patterns; \pkg{rhs} is evaluated for every match. Integration variables in \pkg{rhs} are renamed automatically, so that
the rule can be used several times in one term. Once declared, the rule is used by \fnref{FunctionalDerivative} whenever
it meets the object, including inside other functions (chain rule); the object is treated as depending on the sources of
that head. Several rules can be declared for the same object and different sources. The rules are removed by
\fnref{ResetDeclarations}. Section~\ref{sec:derivatives} has an example; \fnref{ConnectedFunctionFromVertices} uses the same mechanism
internally.

\section{Functional derivatives and canonical form}\label{sec:derivatives}

\subsection{The derivative}

\begin{function}{FunctionalDerivative}
\syntax{FunctionalDerivative[F, s1, s2, \dots, sn]}
\syntax{FunctionalDerivative[F, \{s1, s2, \dots, sn\}]}
\syntax{FunctionalDerivative[F, G[x, y]]}
\syntax{FunctionalDerivative[F, s1, \dots, sn, "MaximumOrder" -> \{x, n\}]}
\end{function}
The functional derivative
\[
\frac{\delta}{\delta s_1}\frac{\delta}{\delta s_2}\cdots\frac{\delta}{\delta s_n}F ,
\]
the rightmost derivative acting first. The $s_i$ are source atoms such as \pkg{J[x]} or \pkg{eta[x]}, with declared heads
(\fnref{DeclareSource}, \fnref{DeclareGrassmannSource}); their arguments can be any labels, including integration
variables of an enclosing expression. The result is in canonical form (section~\ref{sec:canonical}). The derivative is
computed term by term, with the following rules.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Sources.} $\delta J(x)/\delta J(y)=\delta(x,y)$, with one delta function per argument; sources of other heads
  and objects that do not depend on the source give zero.
\item \emph{Product rule}, for the commuting factors and for the Grassmann-odd factors. Derivatives with respect to
  Grassmann sources are left derivatives: when the derivative reaches the $k$-th odd factor it has passed $k-1$ odd
  factors, and the term gets the sign $(-1)^{k-1}$.
\item \emph{Chain rule}, for powers, exponentials, logarithms and any other function: the derivative of
  \pkg{f[u, v, \dots]} is $\sum_i \partial_i f\,\delta u_i/\delta s$, with $\partial_i f$ written
  \pkg{Derivative[0, \dots, 1, \dots][f][u, v, \dots]} (displayed $f'$, $f''$, \dots{} for functions of one argument).
\item \emph{Integrals}, nested at any depth: the integrand is differentiated, the integration variables being renamed
  first so that they cannot clash with the labels of the source.
\item \emph{Declared rules} (\fnref{DeclareFunctionalDerivative}) for objects that depend on the sources implicitly.
\item \emph{Delta functions} produced by the derivatives are integrated at once against integration variables.
\end{itemize}
Successive derivatives are applied one at a time, each followed by the canonical form, so that equal terms are combined
early. Examples:

\paragraph{Derivatives with respect to two-index objects} The propagator need not be declared as a source:
\pkg{FunctionalDerivative[F, G[x, y]]}, with \pkg{G} any kernel of two labels that is not a source --- a propagator, a
self-energy, an interaction $V$, the propagator in a background --- treats every $G(a,b)$ in \pkg{F} as an independent
variable ($\delta G(a,b)/\delta G(x,y)=\delta(a,x)\,\delta(b,y)$, symmetrized for a symmetric \pkg{G}, antisymmetrized,

free of \pkg{G} gives 0 when \pkg{G} is a known kernel (a declared line, a symmetric head); an undeclared head gives the

\paragraph{Up to an order} The option \opt{MaximumOrder} \pkg{-> \{x, n\}}, written after the sources, keeps the

after each derivative, since a derivative cannot lower their order; a term whose order cannot be read (\pkg{x} in an

\subsection{Canonical form}\label{sec:canonical}

Two terms are the same if one is obtained from the other by renaming its integration variables, by permuting the
arguments of symmetric functions, and by reordering its Grassmann factors with the corresponding sign. The canonical
form of a term is the representative of its class chosen by a fixed rule (section~\ref{sec:algorithms}); in it the
integration variables are \pkg{DummyVariable[depth, k]}, the odd factors are sorted, and the numerical coefficient is in
front. A term that changes sign under a renaming that maps it to itself --- a symmetry of the diagram with an odd
permutation of Grassmann factors, or an antisymmetric function --- is zero. Terms with the same canonical form are
combined, and their weights added.

\begin{function}{SimplifyDiagrams}
\syntax{SimplifyDiagrams[expr]}
\end{function}
Brings \pkg{expr} to canonical form: the delta functions are integrated, the delta functions between external labels are

The interaction is an expression written with the fields, for instance
\begin{code}

\section{Classes of diagrams}\label{sec:classes}

\subsection{The graph of a diagram}\label{sec:graph}

The functions of this section decide the class of a diagram on a graph built from its term. The \emph{points} are the
external labels (the second argument of the functions) and the integration variables. Every factor that is not one of
the \emph{lines} --- the factors with the heads given as last argument, typically the propagators --- glues its points
into one \emph{node}: an interaction line, a vertex function, a delta function. The lines are the edges of the graph.
An external label that appears in a single factor, a line joining it to an integration variable, is an \emph{external
leg}: the leg is not an edge, and the node at its other end is marked as external. Any other external label (one that
sits on a vertex, as in amputated functions) is a point of the graph, and its node is external. Lines from a node to
itself (a tadpole $G(z,z)$) are never cut.

\section{Conventions for many-body problems}\label{sec:conventions}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Fermions.} $G_0(x,y)=-\langle T\psi(x)\bar\psi(y)\rangle_0$, with the free generating functional and the
  fields
\begin{code}
DeclareGrassmannSource[eta, etabar]; DeclareField[psi, etabar]; DeclareField[psibar, eta, -1];
Z0 = Exp[-IntegralOver[G0[x, y] etabar[x] ** eta[y], {x, y}]];
\end{code}
  The Green function, $G=G_0+G_0\Sigma G$, is \pkg{-PerturbativeCorrelationFunction[Z0, \{psi[x], psibar[y]\}, \dots]}, and
  \pkg{G[x, y]} is drawn as an arrow from $y$ to $x$.
\item \textbf{Two-body interaction.}
\begin{code}
DeclareSymmetric[V];
interaction = 1/2 IntegralOver[V[x, y] psibar[x] ** psibar[y] ** psi[y] ** psi[x], {x, y}];
\end{code}
  The labels contain imaginary time (an instantaneous interaction carries
  $\delta(\tau_x-\tau_y)$) and spin: for a spin-independent interaction each closed fermion loop carries the factor $2s+1$
  on evaluation, or spin is summed explicitly (\fnref{DeclareDiscreteIndexValues}). An interaction line stands for $-V$ and a vertex for minus its coupling: the minus
  sign comes from expanding $e^{-S_{\rm int}}$ (section~\ref{sec:weights}). The Hartree self-energy is
  $+\delta(x,y)\int_zV(x,z)G(z,z)$, drawn with the weight $-1$, the Fock self-energy $-G(x,y)V(x,y)$, drawn with the weight
  $+1$: a diagram of $G$ or $\Sigma$ with $n_L$ closed fermion loops has the weight $(-1)^{n_L}$, as in the textbooks,
  and its coefficient is $(-1)^{n+n_L}$ at order $n$.
\item \textbf{Closed fermion loops} carry their factor $-1$ automatically, from the Grassmann algebra; so do the exchange
  terms.
\item \textbf{Vertices.} $\Sigma(x,y)=\delta\Phi/\delta G(y,x)$, $\Gamma^0(x_1,x_2;x_3,x_4)=\delta\Sigma(x_1,x_2)/\delta G(x_4,x_3)$; the
  fermions leave at $x_1$, $x_3$ and enter at $x_2$, $x_4$;
  $\langle\psi_{1'}\bar\psi_{2'}\bar\psi_{4'}\psi_{3'}\rangle_c=G_{1'1}G_{22'}F_{12;34}G_{44'}G_{3'3}$. A pair of
  propagators joining two vertices in a channel is written $L_0$ (never $GG$): in the particle--hole channel
  $L_0(x_1,x_2;x_3,x_4)=G(x_1,x_4)\,G(x_3,x_2)$, so that $F=\Gamma^0+\Gamma^0L_0F$.
\item \textbf{Real bosons.} $G=\langle\phi\phi\rangle$, $Z_0=\exp\bigl(\frac12\int_{xy}J(x)D_0(x,y)J(y)\bigr)$, $G=G_0+G_0\Sigma G$,
  $\Sigma=-2\,\delta\Phi/\delta G$ ($\Phi$ the 2PI part $\Gamma_2$ of the effective action), $F=\Gamma^{(4)}$.
\item \textbf{Superfields.} The Nambu field $\boldsymbol\psi_a=(\psi,\bar\psi)$, a label $a=(x,s)$ with a component: one
  Grassmann source, $Z_0=\exp\bigl(\frac12\int_{ab}J_a\mathcal G_{0,ab}J_b\bigr)$, the antisymmetric propagator $\mathcal G_{ab}=-\langle
  T\boldsymbol\psi_a\boldsymbol\psi_b\rangle$ with the blocks $\mathcal G_{\psi\psi}$, $G=\mathcal G_{\psi\bar\psi}$, $-G^T$, $\mathcal G_{\bar\psi\bar\psi}$, and
  $S_{\rm int}=\frac1{4!}\int\mathcal V_{abcd}\boldsymbol\psi_a\boldsymbol\psi_b\boldsymbol\psi_c\boldsymbol\psi_d$; $\boldsymbol\Sigma=-2\,\delta\Phi/\delta\mathcal G$,
  with the gap functions $\Delta(x,y)=\boldsymbol\Sigma_{x\bar\psi,y\bar\psi}$ and $\bar\Delta(x,y)=\boldsymbol\Sigma_{x\psi,y\psi}$, and
  $\Gamma^0=4\,\delta^2\Phi/\delta\mathcal G\,\delta\mathcal G$, the kernel of the channel $s$. With the legs $(x_1\bar\psi,x_2\psi,x_3\bar\psi,x_4\psi)$
  the channels $s$, $t$, $u$ are $\ph$, $\pp$, $\phbar$.
\item \textbf{Electrons and phonons, disorder.} Further Gaussian fields multiply the free generating functional: a
  phonon field $\varphi$ with propagator $D_0$ and the vertex $g\int_z\varphi(z)\bar\psi(z)\psi(z)$ (\pkg{DeclareCoupling[g]}: in the
  pictures each vertex stands for $-g$); a Gaussian random
  potential $u$ with $\overline{u(x)u(y)}=\gamma(x,y)$ in the same way, the quenched average dropping the closed fermion
  loops (\fnref{DropClosedLoops}). The showcase has the code of both.
\item \textbf{Momentum and frequency.} $G_0(k,i\omega_n)=1/(i\omega_n-\xi_k)$; the density correlation function of the
  package is minus the Lindhard function, and its boson propagator $D_0=\langle\phi\phi\rangle_0$ minus the
  $\mathcal D_0=-\langle T\phi\phi\rangle_0$ of the textbooks (section~\ref{sec:matsubara-conventions}).
\item \textbf{Names.} The same objects have different names in condensed matter, field theory, nuclear physics, quantum
  chemistry and statistical physics: the self-energy is also the mass operator, $\Phi$ the 2PI part $\Gamma_2$ of the
  effective action, $\Gamma^0$ the Bethe--Salpeter kernel, the bubble chain the RPA, and so on. The table \emph{Other names} at the
  beginning of the showcase collects them.
\end{itemize}

\section{Self-energy, Luttinger--Ward functional and vertices}\label{sec:functionals}

\subsection{Conventions}

\begin{itemize}
\item Fermions: $G=-\langle T\psi\bar\psi\rangle$, $G=G_0+G_0\Sigma G$. The Luttinger--Ward functional
  $\Phi[G]$~\cite{LuttingerWard1960,Baym1962} is minus the sum of the two-particle-irreducible connected vacuum diagrams
  with $G_0\to G$; $\Sigma(x,y)=\delta\Phi/\delta G(y,x)$ and
  $\Gamma^0(x_1,x_2;x_3,x_4)=\delta\Sigma(x_1,x_2)/\delta G(x_4,x_3)$. The fermions leave the vertex at $x_1$, $x_3$ and enter

\item Real bosons (option \pkg{"Statistics" -> "Boson"}): $G=\langle\phi\phi\rangle$, $G=G_0+G_0\Sigma G$, $\Phi$ is the
  two-particle-irreducible part $\Gamma_2$ of the effective action, $\Sigma(x,y)=-2\,\delta\Phi/\delta G(x,y)$ and

  $\Gamma^0(x_1,x_2;x_3,x_4)=\delta\Sigma(x_1,x_2)/\delta G(x_4,x_3)$. The channels and the parquet functions are for
  fermions and real bosons only.
\item The derivatives with respect to $G$ are functional derivatives in which \pkg{G} is treated as a source:
  differentiating a diagram opens one of its lines, in all possible ways.
\end{itemize}

\section{Effective actions and operator traces}\label{sec:effective}

The effective actions are objects of the package, written with inert traces of operators and differentiated with respect
to the propagator (section~\ref{sec:derivatives}), to the mean fields and to one-body sources. An operator is a kernel ---
a symbol \pkg{h} standing for $h(x,y)$, or a \fnref{LabelFunction} --- a number (times the identity), a sum, a coefficient
times an operator, a product \pkg{A . B} (\pkg{Dot}), an integer power or an inverse \pkg{A\^{}-1}. The kernel of
\pkg{A . B} is $\int A(x,u)\,B(u,y)$, and ${\rm Tr}(A\,B)=\int A(x,y)\,B(y,x)$: with this trace ${\rm Tr}(\Sigma G)$ is the
$\int\Sigma(x,y)\,G(y,x)$ of \fnref{SelfEnergy}. A single symbol is a kernel; in a product with \pkg{Times} the factor
that contains a declared kernel (\fnref{DeclareDirectedPropagator}, \fnref{DeclareSymmetric}, a source) is the operator
and the others are coefficients, and when this cannot be told (\pkg{lambda S}, neither declared) the message
\pkg{opscalar} is given and the result is \pkg{\$Failed}. A chemical potential is therefore
\pkg{LabelFunction[mu DeltaFunction[a, b], \{a, b\}]}. \pkg{Inverse[A]} is read as \pkg{A\^{}-1}. Write a product with an
inverse as \pkg{G0\^{}(-1) . G}: Mathematica reads \pkg{G0\^{}-1 . G} as \pkg{G0\^{}(-(1 . G))}, a power whose exponent is a
product, which the package takes as \pkg{G0\^{}(-1) . G} with the message \pkg{opdotpower}. A source enters an operator
through a kernel, a \fnref{LabelFunction} or a head whose derivative is declared (\fnref{DeclareFunctionalDerivative}), and
the derivatives of traces and kernels follow it there; a source outside any kernel (\pkg{D0\^{}-1 + lambda/2 phibar\^{}2},
without labels) gives the message \pkg{opsource}, and the derivative is taken as 0.

\section{Two-particle channels, Bethe--Salpeter ladders and the parquet}\label{sec:parquet}

\subsection{Channels}

The four legs of a vertex can be divided into two pairs in three ways~\cite{Bickers2004}; in each channel the
vertex obeys a Bethe--Salpeter equation, and the decomposition follows from the Legendre transforms of the 2PI
formalism~\cite{DeDominicisMartin1964b}. With the fermionic convention (legs $x_1$, $x_3$
outgoing, $x_2$, $x_4$ incoming), and for real bosons:
\begin{center}
\small

\toprule
fermions & & & bosons & \\
\midrule
\opt{ParticleHole} & $\ph$ & $(x_1x_2|x_3x_4)$ & \opt{s} & $(x_1x_2|x_3x_4)$\\
\opt{TransverseParticleHole} & $\phbar$ & $(x_1x_4|x_3x_2)$ & \opt{t} & $(x_1x_3|x_2x_4)$\\
\opt{ParticleParticle} & $\pp$ & $(x_1x_3|x_2x_4)$ & \opt{u} & $(x_1x_4|x_2x_3)$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
A superfield (\pkg{"Statistics" -> "Superfield"}: one Grassmann field with an antisymmetric propagator, such as the
Nambu field $(\psi,\bar\psi)$) has the channels of real bosons, $s$, $t$, $u$. A diagram is reducible in a channel if
cutting two propagators separates its pairs. By the parquet theorem a diagram is
reducible in at most one channel, so that $F=\Gamma_{\rm irr}+\sum_r R_r$, with $\Gamma_{\rm irr}$ fully irreducible (often written
$\Lambda$; here $\Lambda$ is the vertex of the density) and $R_r$ the diagrams
reducible in the channel $r$; $\Gamma_r=F-R_r$ is the vertex irreducible in $r$, and $R_r=\Gamma_r\circ_r F$ (Bethe--Salpeter
equation).

\subsection{The weights}\label{sec:weights}

The number written in front of a picture, times the values of its elements, is the term. A propagator stands for itself
($G(x,y)$ for an arrow from $y$ to $x$); an interaction line (\fnref{DeclareInteractionLine}, or the option
\opt{Interactions}) stands for minus its kernel, $-V$; a local vertex (\fnref{DeclareLocalVertex}) for minus its kernel; a
vertex for minus its coupling (\fnref{DeclareCoupling}): the minus signs come from expanding $e^{-S_{\rm int}}$. The
internal points are integrated over. So the Fock self-energy $-G(x,y)V(x,y)$ has the weight $1$, the Hartree
self-energy $\delta(x,y)\int_zV(x,z)G(z,z)$ the weight $-1$ (the sign of its closed fermion loop), and the tadpole of $\phi^4$
theory, $-\frac\lambda2\int_zD_0(a,z)D_0(z,z)D_0(z,b)$, the weight $\frac12$. Only the weights written with the pictures
follow this convention: the expressions, and \fnref{DiagramTerms}, keep their coefficients, and \pkg{"Weights" ->
"Coefficients"} writes the coefficients themselves.

An anomalous line ($-\langle T\psi\psi\rangle$, $-\langle T\bar\psi\bar\psi\rangle$, styles \opt{DivergingArrows} and
\opt{ConvergingArrows}) and an antisymmetric blob of two labels (a gap function) show no orientation: the weight takes them
read along their fermion line, around a loop in either sense (a loop holds an even number of them, so the sense does not
matter) and along an open line from its end at the first external label (the sense matters only when both ends are of one
kind). So $V(x_1,x_2)\,\mathcal G_{\psi\psi}(x_1,x_2)$, the gap function at first order, has the weight $-1$ with the labels
\pkg{\{x1, x2\}} and $+1$ with \pkg{\{x2, x1\}}, and the pairing term $-\frac12\int_{z_1z_2}V(z_1,z_2)\,\mathcal G_{\psi\psi}(z_1,z_2)\,\mathcal
G_{\bar\psi\bar\psi}(z_1,z_2)$ of the Luttinger--Ward functional, a loop read as $\mathcal G_{\psi\psi}(z_1,z_2)\,\mathcal G_{\bar\psi\bar\psi}(z_2,z_1)$, the
weight $-\frac12$. The undirected lines and the vertex of a superfield are antisymmetric too, but on no fermion line: their
pictures fix a term up to the order of its labels, and the weight refers to the canonical labelling.

\subsection{What is drawn}

\begin{itemize}
\item Two-label factors are lines, drawn in the style of their head (\fnref{DeclareLineStyle}); a directed line
  \pkg{G[x, y]} runs from \pkg{y} to \pkg{x}. Factors with three or more labels are grey blobs (vertex functions), with
  the lines that end on them drawn to the blob. Factors with one label are source insertions, a crossed circle.
\item Labels joined by \pkg{DeltaFunction} are one point.
\item An external label alone at the end of a single line (a propagator or an interaction line) is an external point of a
  correlation function, drawn as an open circle. Any other external label sits on a vertex of an amputated function
  (self-energy, vertex) and gets an external leg. For fermions the leg has an arrow: outgoing if one more fermion line
  enters the vertex than leaves it, incoming in the opposite case, none if as many enter as leave. The directions are
  decided from all the terms of the expression together, so that the legs of a sum are consistent; \opt{LegDirections}
  sets them explicitly.
\item An external label at which a density is inserted (two fields at one point, such as $\bar\psi\psi$ or $\phi^2$: in
  every term of the expression the label is alone at its point, which ends exactly two propagators, one entering and one
  leaving or two undirected ones, and nothing else, and it is not an argument of a local vertex) is the insertion of an
  external field $U(z)$: it is drawn as the crossed circle of a source insertion, without a leg.
  \opt{AmputatedLegs}~\pkg{->}~\pkg{True} or \pkg{False} turns this off.
\item Disconnected parts are drawn side by side: vacuum bubbles next to the rest, and a product of two one-point functions
  (such as $\langle\phi(x)\rangle\langle\phi(y)\rangle$) as two pictures.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Layout}\label{sec:layout}

No coordinate is stored: the layout is computed from the diagram, deterministically, so that the same diagram is always
drawn the same way.

\paragraph{Fermion lines} In a theory of fermions coupled by density-type vertices --- two-body interactions, phonons,
impurities --- every vertex lies on exactly one fermion line: an open line between external points, or a closed loop (a
vertex with a tadpole $G(z,z)$ is a loop by itself). The fermion lines are found by following the directed propagators,
and the diagram is drawn line by line:
\begin{itemize}
\item a two-point function whose external points are the ends of one open line has it on a horizontal axis, the first
  external label on the left; the lines between vertices of the axis are arcs, above or below;
\item a four-point function whose external points are the ends of two open lines has them as two parallel rails. The
  external labels are at the corners of a rectangle, in the order of \opt{ExternalOrder}; each rail joins two neighbouring
  corners, so that the rails are horizontal or vertical according to the pairing of the external points (if a rail would
  join opposite corners, the rails are laid out with the labels of the two right corners exchanged, and the two legs on
  the right cross back as an X; with \texttt{"Uncrossed"} they stay exchanged). The legs point to the corners;
\item the interaction lines between the rails are perpendicular to them, so that ladders are drawn as ladders; two lines
  that cross make a symmetric cross, with more room around it. Arcs between vertices of one rail go outside, or inside
  when that avoids a conflict;
\item every closed loop is a circle with its vertices evenly spaced, or a bubble of two arcs when it has two vertices.
  A bubble joined to two points of a line that are further apart than its width (a dressed interaction line) keeps its
  width and hangs below them, and its two interaction lines are bent into one round arc with it.
  The loops form blocks hanging from the open lines. A loop joined to one vertex of a line hangs from it, pointing away;
  a loop joined to several vertices of a line sits above them, its joined vertices on the lower half of the circle right
  above their points, so that the lines joining them are perpendicular to the line; loops hanging from loops point away
  from their centre; a loop joined to both rails lies between them; chains of loops between two points are straight;
\item the side of every arc and block is chosen by trying all the assignments and counting conflicts: two arcs that cross,
  an arc over a vertex with something on its side, blocks with interleaved attachments, and (less) an arc nested under a
  block, a block at the end of an arc. The positions along the lines follow from the room that what hangs from them needs,
  and they are widened until no two loops overlap;
\item vacuum diagrams made of three or more loops joined as a tree have the most central loop in the middle, turned so
  that as many lines as possible leave it horizontally or vertically, and the others around it.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Trees of vertex functions} A connected function written as a tree of vertex functions joined by propagators,
with the external points at its leaves (the tree expansion, \fnref{ConnectedFunctionFromVertices}), has its central vertex,
or its two central vertices, in the middle. Chains of vertices are straight; the legs of a vertex at the end of a chain
are fanned outwards, those of a vertex along a chain perpendicular to it; a vertex with three or more vertex neighbours
has them all around. The external points follow each other counterclockwise in the order of their labels; a tree of
four points and two vertices whose pairs are the first and the last label is drawn upright, and one that pairs opposite
corners is drawn upright with the two legs on the right crossing, at 60 degrees, clear of the vertices. A tree whose
leaves are free ends (its labels integrated over: the shapes of the trees $W^{(n)}$ under \fnref{ConnectedFunctionFromVertices}) is drawn
alike, the free ends where the external points would be and its centre on the axis; its inner nodes may also be bare
vertices of three or more propagators. Its leaves have no order: the branches of a vertex are arranged by their shapes,
so that a symmetric tree is drawn symmetric about the vertical axis, and the legs along a chain are on its upper side.

\paragraph{Other diagrams} Diagrams of other kinds --- bosonic theories, vertices with more than two fermion lines,
propagators alone --- are drawn by the general layout: the external points of a two-point function on
a horizontal axis with the rest in layers above it; those of an $n$-point function on a circle (at the corners of a
square for four points), counterclockwise from the top left, the open lines between neighbouring external points
straight, and the rest placed by a barycentric embedding (a vertex with two legs at opposite corners, which would be in
the middle with its legs across the picture, is placed with the labels of the right corners exchanged, and the two legs
on the right cross back as an X outside the diagram, at 60 degrees when both leave bubbles, which they flatten into
lenses; a loop through the vertex of such a leg is then drawn with straight lines if its circle would be crossed); vacuum parts as a cycle with the rest inside, or several loops
side by side, around a circle, or one inside the other (two loops joined by three or more lines). Closed fermion loops are
circles in this layout too. When three interaction lines would be diameters of a fermion loop drawn as a circle, meeting at
its centre, one is drawn outside the loop, over the top, and the other two cross at the centre; with four or more the vertices
are moved alternately apart, so that the lines cross in pairs. Two bubbles side by side are joined at the sides facing each other, and where two wavy lines
cross their waves are flattened in the TikZ output.
Closed loops of undirected propagators with three or more vertices are drawn as circles too, and two bubbles that meet
at a vertex as flat lenses. Of two chords of a loop that would cross, one is drawn outside it. A picture without external
points is turned upright along its principal axes.

\paragraph{Symmetry} Among layouts of equal quality the symmetric one is taken, so that symmetric diagrams are drawn
symmetric.

\section{Evaluation: Matsubara sums}\label{sec:matsubara}

The diagrams of the package are functions of labels. For a system that is translation invariant in space and in imaginary
time, at a temperature $T$, they become the familiar expressions in momentum and Matsubara
frequency~\cite{Matsubara1955,Mahan,BruusFlensberg}: every independent loop of a diagram (not only the closed fermion
loops) carries a momentum $p$ and a frequency, and contributes $T\sum_n\int\!\frac{d^dp}{(2\pi)^d}$.
\fnref{EvaluateMatsubara} routes the momenta and frequencies through the lines and carries out the frequency sums by
residues; the momentum integrals are left as \pkg{MomentumSum}. The result
is continued to real frequency by \fnref{AnalyticContinuation}, and \fnref{RetardedImaginaryPart} gives its imaginary part,
which the finite-temperature cutting rules (\fnref{CuttingRuleImaginaryPart}, \fnref{CheckCuttingRules}) compute
independently.

\subsection{Conventions}\label{sec:matsubara-conventions}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Propagators.} $G_0(k,i\omega_n)=1/(i\omega_n-\xi_k)$ and $G(\tau)=T\sum_ne^{-i\omega_n\tau}G(i\omega_n)$, consistent with
  $G_0(x,y)=-\langle T\psi(x)\bar\psi(y)\rangle_0$; a line \pkg{G[x, y]} carries its momentum and frequency from $y$ to $x$.
  The fermionic frequencies are $\omega_n=(2n+1)\pi T$, the bosonic ones $\nu_n=2n\pi T$; a symbol \pkg{w} declared by
  \fnref{DeclareMatsubaraFrequency} stands for one of them and is written \pkg{I w} in expressions.
\item \textbf{Lines.} The rules of \fnref{EvaluateMatsubara} say what each head is: \pkg{G0 -> \{"Fermion", xi\}} for
  $1/(i\omega-\xi(p))$; \pkg{V -> \{"Instantaneous", v\}} for an interaction $V(x,y)=v(\mathbf r_x-\mathbf r_y)\,\delta(\tau_x-\tau_y)$,
  which carries no frequency and becomes $v(q)$; \pkg{D0 -> \{"Boson", Function[\{q, z\}, d]\}} for a boson propagator, a
  rational function $d(q,z)$ of $z=i\nu_n$. Instantaneous and boson lines are even, $v(-q)=v(q)$ and $d(-q,-z)=d(q,z)$.
\item \textbf{Signs.} The package's correlation functions are time-ordered products without the minus sign of the
  response functions. The density correlation $\langle\rho_a\rho_b\rangle_c=-G_0(a,b)\,G_0(b,a)$ is, to lowest order, minus
  the Lindhard function~\cite{Lindhard1954}
  $\chi_0(q,i\nu_n)=(2s+1)\sum_p\bigl(n_F(\xi_p)-n_F(\xi_{p+q})\bigr)/(i\nu_n+\xi_p-\xi_{p+q})$, and the boson propagator
  $D_0=\langle\phi\phi\rangle_0$ of $Z_0=e^{\frac12J\cdot D_0\cdot J}$ is minus the propagator
  $\mathcal D_0=-\langle T\phi\phi\rangle_0$ of the textbooks: for phonons~\cite{Mahan}
  $\mathcal D_0(q,i\nu_n)=2\Omega_q/((i\nu_n)^2-\Omega_q^2)$, so that the line of the package, $D_0=-\mathcal D_0$, is
  \pkg{Function[\{q, z\}, 2 Om[q]/(Om[q]\^{}2 - z\^{}2)]}.
\item \textbf{Convergence factors.} A sum $T\sum_n g(i\omega_n)$ of a summand that decays only like $1/\omega_n$ depends on
  how it is cut off. A propagator at equal labels, $G(z,z)$, and the propagators between labels joined by instantaneous
  interactions are taken at equal times, $G(\tau=0^-)$, with the factor $e^{i\omega_n0^+}$:
  $T\sum_ne^{i\omega_n0^+}/(i\omega_n-\xi)=n_F(\xi)$, the density. \fnref{EvaluateMatsubara} finds these lines from the
  topology of the diagram; \fnref{MatsubaraSum} takes the factor as an option. The factor is the ordering of the
  operators at equal times, and the option \opt{ConvergenceFactor} of \fnref{EvaluateMatsubara} chooses one of three:
  \pkg{1} (the default), $G(0^-)=\langle\psi^\dagger\psi\rangle$, the factor $e^{i\omega_n0^+}$ and $n_F(\xi)$; \pkg{-1},
  $G(0^+)=-\langle\psi\psi^\dagger\rangle$, the factor $e^{-i\omega_n0^+}$ and $n_F(\xi)-1$; \pkg{0}, the mean
  $\frac12\langle[\psi^\dagger,\psi]\rangle$, the symmetric sum without a factor and $n_F(\xi)-\frac12$; a boson line,
  even and continuous at $\tau=0$, needs no factor (\fnref{MatsubaraSum} of a boson pole gives $-n_B$, $-n_B-1$ and
  $-n_B-\frac12$). They differ by a constant per state; the symmetric choice is natural on a lattice with a
  particle--hole symmetric interaction, where the difference is a shift of the chemical potential, while in the continuum
  the sum over states of the constant diverges.
\item \textbf{Statistical factors.} $n_F(\epsilon)=1/(e^{\epsilon/T}+1)$ and $n_B(\epsilon)=1/(e^{\epsilon/T}-1)$ are inert
  (\pkg{FermiFunction}, \pkg{BoseFunction}); a pole of order $k$ gives their derivatives of order $k-1$, as for a self-energy
  insertion on a line. They are periodic in the Matsubara frequencies: $n_F(\epsilon+i\nu_m)=n_F(\epsilon)$,
  $n_F(\epsilon+i\omega_m)=-n_B(\epsilon)$, $n_B(\epsilon+i\omega_m)=-n_F(\epsilon)$; after each sum the declared
  frequencies are removed from the arguments in this way, and every argument is written with its first non-constant term
  positive ($n_F(-\epsilon)=1-n_F(\epsilon)$, $n_B(-\epsilon)=-1-n_B(\epsilon)$); constants such as the chemical
  potential do not count, so that $n_F(\epsilon_p-\mu)$ stays as it is. $n_F(0)=\frac12$ at every temperature.
\item \textbf{Analytic continuation.} Only after the sums, when the statistical factors no longer depend on the external
  frequency, can $i\omega_n$ be continued to $\omega+i0^+$, which gives the retarded function~\cite{FetterWalecka}.
\item \textbf{Form of the results.} Terms with the same denominator are put together; a function of one external
  frequency is written in partial fractions of it (part by part, see \fnref{EvaluateMatsubara}); and the statistical
  factors are written as a sum over processes, every line emitted ($1-n_F$, $1+n_B$) or absorbed ($n_F$, $n_B$), when
  this is shorter, as in the second-order self-energy below.
\item \textbf{Lines.} Fermion lines are directed, $1/(i\omega_n-\xi_k)$, and boson lines even: an antisymmetric kernel
  (an anomalous propagator $-\langle T\psi\psi\rangle$ or a superfield propagator) is refused with the message
  \pkg{EvaluateMatsubara::anomalous}, and an expression of a superfield is evaluated through its components at zero
  anomalous propagators (\fnref{SuperfieldComponents}).
\end{itemize}

\begin{function}{MatsubaraSum}
\syntax{MatsubaraSum[f, z, "Fermion"]}
\syntax{MatsubaraSum[f, z, "Boson", "ConvergenceFactor" -> eta]}
\end{function}
$T\sum_nf(z=i\omega_n)$ over the fermionic or the bosonic Matsubara frequencies, for $f$ rational in $z$ with a
denominator that factors into linear and quadratic factors (propagators), by residues: the sum over the poles $z_j$ of $f$ of
$\operatorname{Res}[f\,n_F](z_j)$ (fermions) or $-\operatorname{Res}[f\,n_B](z_j)$ (bosons). Denominators of degree two in
$z$ (boson propagators) are split into linear factors; residues are Laurent coefficients, computed without
\pkg{Residue}, \pkg{Limit} or \pkg{Apart}. How $f$ decays at
large $z$ is read from the degrees in $z$ of its terms. The option \opt{ConvergenceFactor} matters when $f$ decays like
$1/z$: \pkg{1} for the factor $e^{z0^+}=e^{i\omega_n0^+}$ (as for a density), \pkg{-1} for $e^{-z0^+}=e^{-i\omega_n0^+}$,
\pkg{0} for the symmetric sum; for $f=1/(z-a)$ they give $n_F(a)$, $n_F(a)-1$ and $n_F(a)-\frac12$ (bosons: $-n_B(a)$,
$-n_B(a)-1$ and $-n_B(a)-\frac12$). With the default \pkg{Automatic} the symmetric sum is taken, with the message
\pkg{MatsubaraSum::conv}. A summand that does not decay (the sum diverges: \pkg{MatsubaraSum::diverge}), or whose
denominator is not split into poles, gives a message and \pkg{\$Failed}.

\subsection{Cutting rules}\label{sec:cutting}

The imaginary part of a retarded function is a sum over the ways of cutting the diagram into two parts, the lines that
are cut being on shell~\cite{Cutkosky1960}, each cut with its factors. At finite temperature every cut line carries the factor of its emission
($1-n_F$, $1+n_B$) or absorption ($n_F$, $n_B$), and the inverse process adds the factor $1\pm e^{-\omega/T}$
(fermions, bosons)~\cite{Weldon1983,KobesSemenoff1985,KobesSemenoff1986,LandsmanVanWeert1987,LeBellac1996,KapustaGale2006}.
The package derives these rules in imaginary time, independently of the residues: the points joined by instantaneous
interactions form the time vertices of the diagram; the lines inside a time vertex are static (a fermion line is
$G(\tau=0^-)$, that is $n_F$); the other lines form chains from the vertex where the external frequency enters to the one
where it leaves, all the lines of a chain carrying the same frequency. Each chain is on shell at the poles of its
product of propagators, a double pole (a static self-energy on the line) giving a derivative of the delta function.

\section{Evaluation: dimensional regularization}\label{sec:dimreg}

At zero temperature, in Euclidean space, the diagrams of a translation-invariant theory are integrals over loop
momenta. In $d=4-2\varepsilon$ dimensions their ultraviolet divergences become poles in
$\varepsilon$~\cite{tHooftVeltman1972,BolliniGiambiagi1972}, which minimal subtraction removes.
\fnref{DiagramLoopIntegral} routes the momenta through the lines of the diagrams of the package, as
\fnref{EvaluateMatsubara} does, and gives their loop integrals (\fnref{LoopIntegral}); \fnref{IntegrateLoops} evaluates
them, in closed form in $d$, when their loops can be done one after the other as one-loop integrals; \fnref{EpsilonExpand}
and \fnref{PolePart} expand the results in $\varepsilon$. For the counterterms of a massless theory,
\fnref{InfraredRearrangedIntegral} lets one momentum flow through each diagram so that no infrared divergence appears,
and \fnref{BetaFunctionFromZ} and \fnref{AnomalousDimensionFromZ} give the renormalization-group functions from the
renormalization constants. The renormalization of $\phi^4$ theory to two loops (section~\ref{sec:dimreg-phi4}) uses all
of them.

\subsection{Conventions}\label{sec:dimreg-conventions}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Action.} Euclidean, $S=\int\bigl(\frac12(\partial\phi)^2+\frac12m^2\phi^2\bigr)+S_{\rm int}$, with
  $S_{\rm int}=\frac{\lambda}{4!}\int\phi^4$ for $\phi^4$ theory: the free generating functional \pkg{Z0} and the
  interaction \pkg{Phi4Vertex} of section~\ref{sec:perturbation}. A line is $D_0(q)=1/(q^2+m^2)$,
  \fnref{ScalarPropagator}\pkg{[q, m]} (the rule \pkg{D0 -> \{"Scalar", m\}}). The diagrams have the coefficients of the
  package. The vertex functions are the derivatives of the 1PI action at zero mean field,
  $\Gamma^{(n)}=\delta^n\Gamma/\delta\bar\phi^n$ (\fnref{EffectiveAction1PI}): $\Gamma^{(2)}=G^{-1}=p^2+m^2-\Sigma$, with the
  self-energy $\Sigma$ of $G=G_0+G_0\Sigma G_0+\cdots$, and the four-point vertex $\Gamma^{(4)}=\lambda-\cdots$; the amputated
  1PI parts of the connected functions give the same, $\Sigma$ and minus $\Gamma^{(4)}$ (the check).
\item \textbf{Dimension and measure.} $d=4-2\varepsilon$, and every loop is
  \[
  \int_k\equiv\mu^{2\varepsilon}\Bigl(\frac{e^{\gamma_E}}{4\pi}\Bigr)^{\varepsilon}\int\!\frac{d^dk}{(2\pi)^d},\qquad
  \int_k\frac{1}{(k^2+\Delta)^n}=\frac{e^{\varepsilon\gamma_E}\mu^{2\varepsilon}}{16\pi^2}\,\frac{\Gamma(n-d/2)}{\Gamma(n)}\,
  \Delta^{d/2-n},
  \]
  with $\mu$ = \fnref{RenormalizationScale}. With this measure the minimal subtraction of the poles in $\varepsilon$ is the
  $\overline{\rm MS}$ scheme and $\mu$ its scale: no $\gamma_E$ and no $\log4\pi$ appear in the results, and every loop
  is $\kappa=1/(16\pi^2)$ times a function of $\varepsilon$, of the logarithms $\log(p^2/\mu^2)$, $\log(m^2/\mu^2)$ and of
  ratios of masses and momenta.
\item \textbf{Momenta.} The momentum $p_i$ enters the diagram at the external label $x_i$ (\pkg{\{x1 -> p1, \dots\}}; they
  add up to zero), and a line carries its momentum from its second label to its first, written with a positive first
  term (the lines are even). External momenta are symbols, their squares \pkg{p\^{}2}; the square of a combination is
  \fnref{MomentumSquared}\pkg{[q]}.
\item \textbf{Renormalization.} $\phi_B=Z_\phi^{1/2}\phi$, $m_B^2=Z_{m^2}m^2/Z_\phi$ and
  $\lambda_B=\mu^{2\varepsilon}(e^{\gamma_E}/4\pi)^{\varepsilon}Z_\lambda\lambda$; $\beta=\mu\,d\lambda/d\mu$,
  $\gamma_\phi=\frac12\mu\,d\log Z_\phi/d\mu$ and $\gamma_{m^2}=\mu\,d\log m^2/d\mu$, as
  in~\cite{PeskinSchroeder,ZinnJustin2002,KleinertSchulteFrohlinde2001}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limits, and what would come next}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Nested one-loop integrals only.} At each step a loop momentum must appear in at most two different
  propagators, after at most one change of loop momenta. Not covered: the massive sunset and the massive two-loop vacuum
  integrals; genuine two-loop topologies with three propagators in a loop (the kite of the three-loop $\beta$ function of
  $\phi^4$); one-loop triangles and boxes (needed for $\Gamma^{(4)}$ at generic momenta; in Minkowski space,
  \fnref{IntegrateOneLoop} of section~\ref{sec:qed} writes any one-loop integral as Feynman-parameter integrals, which
  \pkg{N} evaluates); a massive bubble inside a further
  loop (its momentum must be external).
\item \textbf{Numerators} are squares of momenta; there are no tensor numerators $k\cdot p$, $k^\mu k^\nu$ (at one loop, in
  Minkowski space, \fnref{IntegrateOneLoop} of section~\ref{sec:qed} does them).
\item \textbf{Massive bubbles} are in closed form to $\varepsilon^0$ for $a=b=1$ and $a+b=3$; beyond, their coefficients are
  \fnref{FeynmanParameterIntegral}, evaluated numerically.
\item \textbf{Infrared rearrangement.} Only the $Z$ factors, the counterterms left after the subtraction of the
  subdivergences, are physical. The first routing without infrared problems is taken, which is legitimate for the
  ultraviolet counterterms of logarithmically divergent functions; quadratically divergent ones are first made
  logarithmic by a derivative ($\partial/\partial p^2$, or $\partial/\partial m^2$ by \fnref{MassInsertion}).
\end{itemize}
The natural next steps:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Integration by parts}~\cite{ChetyrkinTkachov1981}: identities between integrals of the same topology. One of
  them, the triangle rule, writes the massless two-loop master diagram (the kite, finite in four dimensions, where it is
  $6\zeta(3)/p^2$ in units of $\kappa^2$) as a combination of G-functions; with it the massless two-loop propagator
  integrals, and the three-loop $\beta$ function of $\phi^4$ by infrared rearrangement, are within reach. General two-loop
  integrals with masses need a systematic reduction to master integrals (linear systems over rational functions of $d$).
\item \textbf{Massive two-loop integrals} (the sunset with masses, the vacuum integrals with three masses): closed forms
  in hypergeometric functions, expansions in ratios of masses and momenta, or numerical integration over Feynman
  parameters. The $\overline{\rm MS}$ constants do not need them, the finite parts of massive functions do.
\item \textbf{Tensor numerators}, for gauge theories and fermions, in nested integrals: G-functions with numerators. At one
  loop, the Lorentz indices, the Dirac algebra in $d$ dimensions and the tensor integrals are in section~\ref{sec:qed}.
\end{itemize}

\section{Evaluation: QED at one loop}\label{sec:qed}

Quantum electrodynamics is a theory of the package like any other: the electron and the photon are the fields of a
Gaussian generating functional, and the vertex is a local kernel. A label stands for a point together with an index ---
the spinor index of an end of an electron line, the Lorentz index of an end of a photon line --- so that the diagrams of
the package, with their weights and the signs of their closed fermion loops, are the Feynman diagrams of QED in
condensed notation. \fnref{QEDIntegrand} writes them in momentum space: it routes the momenta through the lines, as
\fnref{EvaluateMatsubara} and \fnref{DiagramLoopIntegral} do, and follows the spinor indices along the fermion lines, so
that an open line becomes a product of Dirac matrices and a closed loop a trace. \fnref{DiracSimplify} does the Dirac
algebra in $d$ dimensions, \fnref{IntegrateOneLoop} the loop integral by Feynman parameters, exactly in $d$;
\fnref{ReduceParameterIntegrals} and \fnref{ExpandParameterIntegrals} do the integrals over the Feynman parameters in
closed form where they can and expand in $\varepsilon$, and \fnref{VertexFormFactors} splits a vertex into its form
factors. The electron self-energy, the vacuum polarization and the vertex at one loop (section~\ref{sec:qed-results})
use all of them; \pkg{Tests/QEDOneLoop.wl} is the same computation as a script, which takes about ten seconds.

\subsection{Conventions}\label{sec:qed-conventions}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dirac algebra.} Minkowski metric $(+,-,-,-)$, $\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\}=2g^{\mu\nu}$, $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf 1=4$,
  in $d$ dimensions: $g^\mu{}_\mu=d$. The Lorentz indices are abstract: an index that appears twice in a term is
  contracted (one upper, one lower; in the formulas of the package both are written upper), and there are no explicit
  components. There is no $\gamma_5$.
\item \textbf{Action and Feynman rules}, as in~\cite{PeskinSchroeder} but with $e>0$: the electron has the charge $-e$,
  $\mathcal L=\bar\psi(i\slashed\partial-m)\psi-\frac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+e\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi A_\mu$ (Peskin and Schroeder
  write $-e\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi A_\mu$ with $e<0$ for the electron), so that the vertex is $+ie\gamma^\mu$, the electron line $i(\slashed p+m)/(p^2-m^2+i0)$ and the photon line, in a covariant gauge,
  \[
  \frac{-i}{q^2-\lambda^2+i0}\Bigl(g^{\mu\nu}-(1-\xi)\,\frac{q^\mu q^\nu}{q^2-\lambda^2}\Bigr),
  \]
  with $\xi=1$ in the Feynman gauge and $\xi=0$ in the Landau gauge; a photon mass $\lambda$ is an infrared regulator
  ($\lambda=0$ by default).
\item \textbf{The theory in condensed notation.} With the propagators $S(x,y)=\langle T\psi(x)\bar\psi(y)\rangle_0$ (its row
  is the spinor index of $x$, its column that of $y$) and $D(x,y)=\langle TA(x)A(y)\rangle_0$, the free generating
  functional is $Z_0=\exp\bigl(\frac12\int_{xy}J(x)D(x,y)J(y)+\int_{xy}S(x,y)\,\bar\eta(x)\eta(y)\bigr)$, the electron field
  $\psi$ is $\delta/\delta\bar\eta$ and $\bar\psi$ is $-\delta/\delta\eta$. The package expands $e^{-S_{\rm int}}$; for the
  $e^{iS}$ of Minkowski space, with $S_{\rm int}=e\int\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi A_\mu$, the interaction is
  $-iS_{\rm int}=e\int_{xyz}V(x,y,z)\,\bar\psi(x)\psi(y)A(z)$ with the local
  kernel $V(x,y,z)=-i\gamma^{\mu_z}_{a_xa_y}\delta(x-y)\delta(x-z)$ (the rule \pkg{V -> \{"Vertex", -I\}}), so that each vertex
  is $(-1)\,e\,(-i\gamma^\mu)=ie\gamma^\mu$. The 1PI amputated functions of the package are then $-i\Sigma(p)$,
  $i\Pi^{\mu\nu}(q)$ and $ie\,\delta\Gamma^\mu(p_2,p_1)$, the diagrams of~\cite{PeskinSchroeder} with $e\to-e$; $\Sigma$,
  $\Pi$, the form factors and $\beta(e)$, even in $e$ or of one sign for both, are those of~\cite{PeskinSchroeder}.
\item \textbf{Dimension and measure.} $d=4-2\varepsilon$, and every loop has the measure of
  section~\ref{sec:dimreg-conventions}, in Minkowski space: $\int_k\equiv\mu^{2\varepsilon}(e^{\gamma_E}/4\pi)^\varepsilon\int
  d^dk/(2\pi)^d$, so that minimal subtraction is the $\overline{\rm MS}$ scheme. After the Wick rotation,
  \[
  \int_l\frac{(l^2)^J}{(l^2-\Delta)^A}=i\,(-1)^{A+J}\,\frac{e^{\varepsilon\gamma_E}\mu^{2\varepsilon}}{16\pi^2}\,
  \frac{\Gamma(J+d/2)\,\Gamma(A-J-d/2)}{\Gamma(d/2)\,\Gamma(A)}\,\Delta^{d/2+J-A},
  \]
  with $\Delta-i0$. Peskin and Schroeder write $d=4-\epsilon$: their $2/\epsilon-\gamma_E+\log4\pi+\log\mu^2$ is
  $1/\varepsilon+\log\mu^2$ here.
\item \textbf{Momenta.} The momentum $p_i$ enters the diagram at the external label $x_i$ (\pkg{\{x1 -> p1, \dots\}}; they
  add up to zero), and a line \pkg{S[x, y]} carries its momentum from $y$ to $x$, along the arrow of the electron. A
  momentum is a linear combination of momentum names --- symbols, or objects such as \pkg{p[1]} and \pkg{LoopMomentum[i]}
  --- with coefficients that are numbers or polynomials in the Feynman parameters.
\item \textbf{Renormalization}, as in~\cite[section~10.3]{PeskinSchroeder}: $\psi_0=Z_2^{1/2}\psi$, $A_0=Z_3^{1/2}A$,
  $m_0=Z_mm$, $e_0=\mu^\varepsilon Z_1Z_2^{-1}Z_3^{-1/2}e$ (with the factor $(e^{\gamma_E}/4\pi)^{\varepsilon/2}$ of the measure),
  $\delta_i=Z_i-1$. The self-energy is $\Sigma=m\,A(p^2)+\slashed p\,B(p^2)$, and the counterterm adds
  $i(\slashed p\,\delta_2-\delta_m)$ to $-i\Sigma$, with $\delta_m=Z_2m_0-m$: in $\overline{\rm MS}$, $\delta_2$ is the pole
  of $B$, $\delta_m$ minus $m$ times the pole of $A$, and $Z_m-1=\delta_m/m-\delta_2$. The vacuum polarization is
  $\Pi^{\mu\nu}=(q^2g^{\mu\nu}-q^\mu q^\nu)\,\Pi(q^2)$ and $\delta_3$ the pole of $\Pi$. Between on-shell spinors,
  $\bar u(p_2)\Gamma^\mu u(p_1)=\bar u(p_2)\bigl(\gamma^\mu F_1+\frac{i\sigma^{\mu\nu}q_\nu}{2m}F_2+q^\mu F_3\bigr)u(p_1)$,
  with $q=p_2-p_1$ and $\sigma^{\mu\nu}=\frac i2[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu]$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Dirac algebra}

\begin{function}{GammaMatrix}
\syntax{GammaMatrix[mu]}
\end{function}
The Dirac matrix $\gamma^\mu$ in $d$ dimensions, written $\gamma^\mu$. The index is abstract: an index that appears twice

\end{function}

\begin{function}{ReduceParameterIntegrals}
\syntax{ReduceParameterIntegrals[expr]}
\end{function}

\subsection{Limits, and what would come next}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{One loop.} \fnref{QEDIntegrand} and \fnref{IntegrateOneLoop} take a single loop momentum; diagrams and
  integrals with more loops give the messages \pkg{QEDIntegrand::loops} and \pkg{IntegrateOneLoop::loops}.
\item \textbf{No $\gamma_5$}: no traces with $\gamma_5$, no anomalies, no weak interactions (\pkg{GammaMatrix[5]} is refused).
\item \textbf{At most one open fermion line} per term: no four-fermion amplitudes (M{\o}ller and Bhabha scattering at tree
  level or at one loop, \pkg{QEDIntegrand::spinor}).
\item \textbf{Closed forms} only where $\Delta$ is a monomial in the Feynman parameters (on shell, and the vertex at $q^2=0$);
  otherwise the integrals stay inert and \pkg{N} evaluates them: $\Pi(q^2)$, $\Sigma(p^2)$ off shell and $F_{1,2}(q^2)$ have no
  closed form yet. Symbolic closed forms use the principal branch: $c^a$ and the logarithms that \fnref{EpsilonExpand} writes
  outside an integral are right below thresholds, for positive coefficients. Above a threshold, only \pkg{N} applies the
  prescription $\Delta-i0$; a power $\le-1$ of a base that vanishes inside the region (the vertex at timelike $q^2>4m^2$, finite and
  complex there) is not evaluated by \pkg{N} (\pkg{::threshold}, \pkg{Indeterminate}).
\item \textbf{Infrared divergences} under the integral sign are subtracted at one face of the simplex only
  (\pkg{ExpandParameterIntegrals::ir} otherwise).
\end{itemize}
The natural next steps: the closed forms of the one-dimensional integrals of $\Pi(q^2)$ and $\Sigma(p^2)$ (logarithms and the
square root of the K\"all\'en function, as for the massive bubble of section~\ref{sec:dimreg}), with their continuation above
threshold; $\gamma_5$, naively anticommuting or in the scheme of 't~Hooft and Veltman~\cite{tHooftVeltman1972}; several open
fermion lines, for the boxes of M{\o}ller and Bhabha scattering; and two loops, by the nested integration of
section~\ref{sec:dimreg} with tensor numerators.

\section{Algorithms and performance}\label{sec:algorithms}

\paragraph{Representation} Internally a term is \pkg{diagramTerm[c, odd, dummies]}: the commuting part, the list of
Grassmann-odd factors in order, and the integration variables. An expression is a list of terms; \pkg{Plus},
\pkg{Times}, \pkg{**}, powers and integrals are expanded into such lists, the integration variables being renamed with
fresh symbols.

\paragraph{Differentiation} The derivative of a term is the sum over its commuting factors (product rule, with the chain
rule inside them) and over its odd factors (with the Grassmann sign). Derivatives of source atoms give delta functions,
integrated at once against the integration variables; delta functions between external labels are normalized so that
every group of labels joined by delta functions is represented by its first label.

\paragraph{Canonical form} A canonical renaming of the integration variables is computed by colour refinement: every
variable starts with the same colour, and is then characterized by the multiset of factors it appears in, the other
variables being replaced by their colours, iteratively until the partition is stable. Ties are broken by
individualization, branching over the members of the first non-trivial colour class, as in graph-isomorphism
algorithms~\cite{McKayPiperno2014}.
Before branching, the transpositions within the class are tested: if all of them map the term to itself (with sign
$+1$) a single branch suffices; if one maps it to minus itself, the term vanishes. Among the renamings found, the one
giving the smallest expression in Mathematica's canonical order is chosen. Nested integrals are canonicalised
recursively, with \pkg{DummyVariable[depth, k]}.

\paragraph{Wick's theorem} The pairings are enumerated recursively, from the first unpaired field. When the partner of a
field belongs to an interaction vertex not yet touched, only the first such vertex of each kind and one field per orbit of
the vertex's symmetries (permutations of its integration variables that leave it unchanged, and identical commuting
fields) are tried, with the corresponding multiplicity; identical commuting fields of a vertex already touched are
interchangeable. The connectivity of the partial pairing is tracked with a union--find structure, and components that
close without external points are discarded when only diagrams without vacuum bubbles, or connected ones, are wanted.
Every contraction $\langle f_if_j\rangle_0$ is computed once from $Z_0$, by differentiation, and cached.

\paragraph{Cuts} Irreducibility, skeletons, channels and loops are decided on the graph of section~\ref{sec:graph}, by
trying all the cuts of one or two lines.

\paragraph{Frequency sums} Momenta and frequencies are routed along a spanning tree of the diagram, chosen with the
instantaneous and boson lines first, so that every loop runs along fermion lines; the points joined by instantaneous
lines form the time vertices, and the fermion lines inside a time vertex are at equal times. The poles of a summand are
read from the linear factors of its denominators (a boson propagator is split into two), and its residues, of any
order, are Laurent coefficients computed by differentiation; whether the sum converges, and whether it depends on the
convergence factor, is read from the degrees in $z$ of its terms. After each sum the statistical factors are freed of
the other frequencies by periodicity. At the end the terms are put over common denominators, in partial fractions in the
external frequency (part by part, between the lines that carry it alone), and the statistical factors are written as a
sum over processes when this is shorter. The cutting rules are derived independently, in imaginary time: the
propagating lines between the two external time vertices are grouped into chains, the product of the propagators of
each chain is expanded in partial fractions of its frequency, and each pole puts the chain on shell with its factor of
emission or absorption (section~\ref{sec:cutting}).

\paragraph{Dimensional regularization} The momenta are routed as for the Matsubara sums, every line becoming a
\pkg{ScalarPropagator}. The integrand of a loop integral is expanded into products of propagators, and in each product
the loops are integrated one at a time, by a loop momentum that appears in at most two different propagators (after a
change of variables, if needed); each step is a closed form in $d$ (tadpoles, G-functions with a new massless line of
non-integer power, bubbles), and each product records what it met: scaleless integrals, power or logarithmically
divergent, infrared divergences, irreducible integrals, numerators. These records give the messages of
\fnref{IntegrateLoops} and the choice of a routing in \fnref{InfraredRearrangedIntegral}. The expansion in $\varepsilon$ uses
truncated Laurent series of its own, $\{v,\{c_0,c_1,\dots\}\}$ for $\sum_ic_i\varepsilon^{v+i}$, multiplied, inverted and
exponentiated term by term, the order of every factor being chosen from the poles of the others, so that the result is
exact to the order asked without \pkg{Series}; the Gamma functions are expanded through their logarithmic derivatives,
and the logarithms of a massive bubble are integrated over its Feynman parameter in closed form.

\paragraph{QED at one loop} \fnref{QEDIntegrand} routes the momenta as \fnref{DiagramLoopIntegral} does, with the local
vertices replaced by delta functions between their labels, and builds the fermion lines from pieces \{row, column,
matrix\}, one for every electron line and vertex: an open line is followed from the label that is not a column, a
closed loop from any of its pieces. The Dirac algebra rewrites products by linearity and contracts an index by
$\gamma^\mu X\slashed a\gamma_\mu=2\slashed aX-(\gamma^\mu X\gamma_\mu)\slashed a$; traces are expanded recursively along their
first element and memoized during one call; in a spinor chain $\slashed p{}_1$ is moved to the right and $\slashed p{}_2$ to
the left by anticommutation. Compound momenta, the loop momentum and the Feynman parameters are replaced by symbols
during the algebra. \fnref{IntegrateOneLoop} groups the terms of a numerator by their denominators, introduces the Feynman
parameters, shifts the loop momentum, reduces the tensor numerators by the sum over the pairings of their indices and
applies the master formula term by term; the coefficients of each Dirac and tensor structure are collected into one
Feynman-parameter integral. Before \fnref{ExpandParameterIntegrals} expands under the integral sign, the behaviour of the
integrand near every face of the simplex is read from the orders of its bases there; a singular face is subtracted with
the leading terms of the integrand in the distance from it, integrated exactly (Beta functions times an integral over
the face). \pkg{N} maps the simplex to the unit cube, takes $\Delta-i0$ for the bases that change sign, and splits a
one-dimensional interval at their real roots.

\paragraph{Performance} The showcase --- the electron gas and $\phi^4$ theory to third order ($G$: 87
diagrams, $\Sigma$, $\log Z$, $\Phi$, $\Sigma[G]$, $\Gamma^0$: 58, the full vertex $F$: 94, the parquet checks), the exact
equations, Hubbard--Stratonovich, the Matsubara sums, $\phi^4$ theory to two loops in dimensional regularization, QCD and
the trees of vertex functions up to six points, with several hundred pictures --- runs in about four minutes.
The QED computation of section~\ref{sec:qed-results} (\pkg{Tests/QEDOneLoop.wl}) takes about ten seconds.
The superfield costs more per order than the electron gas: its Luttinger--Ward functional takes about 3~s at third
order and 2~minutes at fourth, \fnref{EffectiveAction2PI} with its mean about 3~minutes at fourth order (16~s for the
fermionic $\Gamma[\bar\Psi,\Psi,G]$), and \fnref{SuperfieldComponents} of that action about 8~minutes.

\item \pkg{FunctionalDiagrams::label}: an external label does not occur in the expression --- a misprint, or the

\item \pkg{FunctionalDiagrams::labelsymbol}: a label is not a symbol (a number, for instance).
\item \pkg{FunctionalDiagrams::optx}: an unknown option of a drawing function or of a function of

\paragraph{Common pitfalls}
\begin{itemize}
\item Labels, integration variables and the heads of functions, sources and fields must be symbols, and not built-in
  ones: \pkg{C}, \pkg{D}, \pkg{E}, \pkg{I}, \pkg{K}, \pkg{N}, \pkg{O}, \pkg{Gamma}, \pkg{Beta}, \pkg{GreenFunction} are built in
  (\pkg{Context[name]} is \pkg{"System`"} for a built-in symbol).
\item Grassmann-odd factors must be multiplied with \pkg{**}, and their order matters.
\item The order of the declarations does not matter: a field may be declared before its source.
\item In a terminal session results print in output form, for instance \pkg{IntegralOver[\dots, \{z1, z2\}]} and
  \pkg{DeltaFunction[a, b]}; the compact forms, $\int_{z_1z_2}$ and $\delta_{ab}$, are shown in notebooks. In a script
  (\pkg{wolfram -script}) \pkg{Print} writes the input form, with the integration variables as \pkg{DummyVariable[1, 1]}:
  \pkg{Print[ToString[expr]]} writes \pkg{z1}.
\item In a script a statement ends at the end of a line where it is complete: \pkg{a = b} on one line and
  \pkg{+ c} on the next are two statements. Break long lines after a binary operator, not before it.
\item A product with an inverse is \pkg{G0\^{}(-1) . G}: \pkg{G0\^{}-1 . G} is read as \pkg{G0\^{}(-(1 . G))} (the package
  corrects it, with the message \pkg{opdotpower}).
\item Long computations can exceed the default \pkg{\$IterationLimit} of Mathematica (4096); set
  \pkg{\$IterationLimit = 10\^{}8} before them, as the scripts of the package do.
\item Results are in canonical form, with the integration variables renamed: compare expressions with
  \pkg{SimplifyDiagrams[a - b] === 0}, not by their appearance.
\item A symmetric function must be declared symmetric before it is used: \pkg{DeclareSymmetric} gives it the attribute
  \pkg{Orderless}, which only affects expressions evaluated afterwards.
\end{itemize}
